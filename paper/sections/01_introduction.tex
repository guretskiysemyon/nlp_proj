%=====================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%=====================================================================

As language models (LMs) are deployed in more applications, the ability to erase specific knowledge from them becomes important for safety and compliance \citep{eldan2023harry,li2024wmdp}.
A prominent approach is \emph{persistent} concept erasure, which removes knowledge of a target concept by modifying the model's weights \citep{gurarieh2025precise,ashuach2026crisp}.
Recently, \citet{suslik2026ember} showed that such methods overlook a part of the model that stores knowledge: the token embeddings.
Their method, \ember{}, decomposes the embeddings of concept-related tokens into sparse features, selects the features of the target concept with an LLM judge, and subtracts them from a small set of tokens.\footnote{Our code is available at \url{https://github.com/guretskiysemyon/nlp_proj}.}
Combined with MLP-based methods, it substantially improves robustness to relearning.

However, an embedding edit can only remove what its features capture, and how reliably \ember{} finds a concept's features has not been studied.
Its authors note that embeddings may not be an effective target for some concepts, that the tokens selected for editing may include semantically unrelated ones, and that the edit only applies to English tokens \citep[Limitations]{suslik2026ember}.

In this work, we hypothesize that these limitations stem in part from the geometry in which \ember{} searches for features.
A concept is carried by rare tokens, and in the raw embedding space rare tokens share a large common component, along the mean of all embeddings.
\ember{}'s factorization therefore groups the concept with other rare tokens: the Harry Potter feature also collects Nestl\'e, Lego and Trump.
Once this shared component is removed, the concept's tokens form a clean cluster, but a \emph{quiet} one, of ordinary value to a factorization that minimizes reconstruction error, so it is often not found at all (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}).
Two more design choices compound the problem.
Every feature keeps the same 1\% of the tokens regardless of how broad its concept is, and the judge accepts a feature based on a one-sentence summary that often abstracts the concept away.
When no feature is accepted, nothing is erased.

To tackle this gap, we introduce \cinder{} (Contrastive INformed Directional Embedding Removal; Figure~\ref{fig:overview}), which keeps \ember{}'s localize-then-edit framework and changes how the concept is found.
\cinder{} whitens the embeddings against ordinary text, which removes the shared component, and sharpens them toward the concept's page, which makes the now clean concept cluster loud.
It shares one sparsity budget across all features, so that each feature takes as many tokens as the data supports, and it verifies features both bottom-up, with \ember{}'s judge, and top-down, against a concept lexicon generated once per concept.
Because the sharpened space is centred on ordinary text, \cinder{}'s edit removes only what distinguishes the concept; an uncentred variant, \cinderz{}, also removes part of the component that all tokens share, as \ember{}'s edit does.

Sharpening also makes multilingual erasure practical, which \ember{} leaves to future work \citep[Limitations]{suslik2026ember}.
Because \ember{} edits the rows of English tokens, a concept it erases in English largely survives in other languages: on Gemma, edited models retain \expnum{0.19--0.24} more of the concept's knowledge in German, Spanish, French and Russian than in English.
Running \ember{} separately in every language multiplies its factorizations and judge calls by the number of languages, and fitting it once on multilingual text mostly fails: it accepts a concept feature for only \expnum{10 of 38} concepts.
In a multilingual matrix the concept is quieter still, since language identity carries far more energy than the concept does, and \ember{}'s judge rejects most of the concept features that the fit does contain.
\cinder{} whitens against a background with equal text in every language, which weakens the language directions, sharpens toward the concept's pages in all languages, and verifies features with its lexicon as well.
A single \cinder{} fit over eight languages, with the uncentred edit of \cinderz{}, then erases as well as eight separate \ember{} runs, with one factorization and one judging pass instead of eight, and an eighth of the judge calls; to our knowledge, this is the first multilingual erasure at the embedding level.

We assess \cinder{} through experiments on Gemma-2-2B-it \citep{gemma2024gemma2} and, across eight languages, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \citep{grattafiori2024llama3}.
Notably, evaluating it exposed a blind spot in the standard protocol.
\ember{}'s tuning score prefers edits up to a hundred times longer than the embeddings they change, which destroy the edited words rather than remove the concept from them.
Because the concept questions contain the edited words, a destroyed word counts as erased knowledge, while MMLU and Alpaca coherence rarely encounter it.
We therefore extend the evaluation with \emph{collateral} questions that use the edited words in their other meanings (e.g., Prince Harry when erasing Harry Potter), one scale of edit size shared by all methods, and tests of whether the edited words survive.

Our results show that once collateral damage is charged for, every method moves from token-destroying edits to edits about as long as the token, and that embedding erasure blocks access to a concept through its words rather than removing it from the weights.
Within this regime, \cinder{} erases concepts that \ember{} misses (\expnum{14} vs.\ \expnum{3} runs in which only one of the methods edits), and at a moderate, equal edit size its direction removes more (\expnum{$+0.15$} on the combined score, \expnum{10 of 11} held-out concepts); this advantage vanishes at larger edits, and at each method's own operating point the two tie.
Across eight languages, a single \cinderz{} fit matches running \ember{} separately in each language (\expnum{0.23} vs.\ \expnum{0.25}) with an eighth of the judge calls, and finds concept features for twice as many concepts as a single multilingual \ember{} fit (\expnum{21} vs.\ \expnum{10} of 38).

Finally, we analyze why the sharpened matrix finds the concept.
Across 38 concepts, sharpening turns the concept from a feature that is valuable but mixed with unrelated rare tokens into one that is both pure and valuable, and finds it in more runs; global sparsity then sets its width close to where the concept's tokens actually end.
The gain is not an artefact of sharpening toward the very text being scored: sharpening toward other pages about the concept keeps it, while another concept's page destroys the feature.
We also find that the model's first layer reads the mean of all embeddings strongly, and that this shared direction, not the concept, explains why \ember{}'s edits are read more strongly than \cinder{}'s.
Concepts that live between broader topics, such as Stoicism, remain out of reach for every method.

To conclude, our work (1) diagnoses why embedding-level erasure misses quiet concepts, (2) introduces \cinder{}, which finds and erases them more reliably, (3) extends embedding-level erasure to many languages with a single fit, which needs an eighth of the judge calls and a fifth of the factorization work of erasing each language separately, and (4) shows that the standard evaluation of embedding erasure rewards destroying tokens, and proposes a collateral-aware alternative that needs no LLM judge inside the tuning loop.
Overall, our findings suggest that where a concept is looked for matters as much as how it is removed, and that an embedding edit must be judged by what it does to the words it edits.
