%=====================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%=====================================================================

As language models (LMs) are deployed more widely, erasing specific knowledge from them becomes important for safety and compliance \citep{eldan2023harry,li2024wmdp}.
\emph{Persistent} concept erasure does so by modifying the model's weights \citep{gurarieh2025precise,ashuach2026crisp}, and \citet{suslik2026ember} showed that such methods overlook a part of the model that stores knowledge: the token embeddings.
Their method, \ember{}, decomposes the embeddings of concept-related tokens into sparse features, selects the concept's features with an LLM judge, and subtracts them from a small set of tokens; combined with MLP-based methods, it substantially improves robustness to relearning.\footnote{Our code is available at \url{https://github.com/guretskiysemyon/nlp_proj}.}

However, an embedding edit can only remove what its features capture, and how reliably \ember{} finds them has not been studied \citep[Limitations]{suslik2026ember}.
We hypothesize that the problem lies partly in the geometry \ember{} searches: concept tokens are rare, and rare tokens share a large component along the mean embedding of ordinary text, so \ember{} groups the concept with other rare tokens: its Harry Potter feature also collects Nestl\'e, Lego and Trump.
Whitened, the concept forms a clean but \emph{quiet} cluster, of ordinary value to a factorization that minimizes reconstruction error (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}).
A fixed feature width and a judge that reads only a one-sentence summary lose more, and when no feature is accepted, nothing is erased.

We introduce \cinder{} (Contrastive INformed Directional Embedding Removal; Figure~\ref{fig:overview}), which keeps \ember{}'s localize-then-edit framework and changes how the concept is found.
It whitens the embeddings against ordinary text, removing the shared component, and sharpens them toward the concept's page, making the clean cluster loud; it lets each feature take as many tokens as the data supports; and it verifies features both bottom-up, with \ember{}'s judge, and top-down, against a lexicon generated once per concept.
Its edit removes only what distinguishes the concept from ordinary text, while the variant \cinderz{}, like \ember{}, also removes part of what all tokens share.

Sharpening also makes multilingual erasure practical, which \ember{} leaves to future work.
\ember{} edits English token rows, so the concept survives in other languages; running it per language multiplies its cost by the number of languages, and a single \ember{} fit on multilingual text accepts a concept feature for only \expnum{10 of 38} concepts, mostly because its judge rejects the concept features the fit finds.
\cinder{} whitens against a background balanced across languages, sharpens toward the concept's pages in all of them and adds its lexicon judge, and on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \citep{grattafiori2024llama3} a single \cinderz{} fit over eight languages comes within \expnum{0.02} of eight separate \ember{} runs, with an eighth of the judge calls: to our knowledge, the first multilingual erasure at the embedding level.

Evaluating \cinder{} on Gemma-2-2B-it \citep{gemma2024gemma2} exposed a blind spot in the standard protocol: \ember{}'s tuning score prefers edits up to a hundred times longer than the embeddings they change, which destroy the edited words, and since the concept questions contain these words, a destroyed word counts as erased knowledge.
We add \emph{collateral} questions that use the edited words in other meanings (e.g., Prince Harry when erasing Harry Potter), a common scale of edit size, and tests of whether the edited words survive.
Once collateral damage is charged for, every method moves to edits about as long as the token, and embedding erasure turns out to block access to a concept through its words rather than remove it from the weights.
In this regime, \cinder{} erases concepts that \ember{} misses (\expnum{14} vs.\ \expnum{3} runs in which only one method edits) and, at a moderate equal edit size, removes more (\expnum{$+0.15$} on the combined score, \expnum{10 of 11} held-out concepts); at each method's own operating point, the two tie.

To conclude, our work (1) diagnoses why embedding-level erasure misses quiet concepts; (2) introduces \cinder{}, which finds and erases them more reliably, with a lexicon judge that needs one LLM call per concept; (3) extends embedding-level erasure to eight languages with a single fit and an eighth of the judge calls; and (4) shows that the standard evaluation rewards destroying tokens, and proposes a collateral-aware alternative with no LLM judge in the tuning loop.
